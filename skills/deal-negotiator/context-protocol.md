# Context Protocol — No Rock Unturned

Most negotiation failures are *context* failures, not *tactics* failures. The negotiator who knows the counterparty's actual deadline, their internal politics, their last failed deal, and their boss's recent comments — that negotiator wins, even with mediocre tactics. The negotiator with brilliant tactics but no context loses.

This file is the interrogation playbook. Run it BEFORE giving the user tactical advice on any deal worth more than $10K of value at stake.

---

## How to use this file

The protocol has three tiers, sized to the deal:

| Tier | When to run | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Quick (5 questions)** | < $25K, simple back-and-forth, repeat counterparty | 5 quick checks |
| **Standard (15 questions)** | $25K-$250K, new counterparty, contract negotiation | Full intake |
| **Deep (30+ questions)** | Above $250K, equity, real estate, cross-border, legal exposure | Standard + Deep dive + counterparty research |

**Important — actually ASK these questions.** Don't assume the answers. Don't infer them from the email. The user knows more about their deals than you'll ever extract from a written context window. The interrogation is where the Black Swans surface.

Use the AskUserQuestion tool to batch questions in groups of 3-4 with structured options when applicable. Don't dump all 15 questions in one wall of text — that's exhausting. Break into logical clusters.

---

## TIER 1 — Quick (5 questions)

Use for: low-stakes follow-ups, ongoing negotiations where you already have context, simple terms revisions.

1. **What is the specific ask or response you need to make?** (One sentence.)
2. **What's your best alternative if this deal dies?** (BATNA in one line.)
3. **What's the deadline pressure on each side?** (Yours / theirs / who needs it more.)
4. **What's the relationship value going forward?** (One-time / repeat / strategic.)
5. **What's the single biggest thing you're worried about getting wrong here?**

If any answer is "I don't know" — bump up to Tier 2.

---

## TIER 2 — Standard (15 questions, grouped)

Use for: most meaningful negotiations.

### Cluster A — The deal itself (5 questions)
1. What is the deal in one sentence? (Strip the jargon. "I'm trying to negotiate X with Y so that Z.")
2. What outcome would you call "great"? "Good enough"? "Walk away"?
3. What's your current ask vs. what they've currently offered?
4. What are ALL the levers in this deal, not just the headline number? (Price, timing, scope, term, exclusivity, IP, performance, payment, termination, contingencies.)
5. What's the realistic best-case outcome? Worst-case? Median expected outcome?

### Cluster B — Leverage map (5 questions)
6. What's your BATNA — *specifically*? If this deal dies, what will you do? (If "figure it out" — fix your BATNA before negotiating.)
7. What's their BATNA, as best you can model it? What happens to them if this deal dies?
8. Who has more time pressure? (Be honest — if you're the one with a hard deadline, that's important to know.)
9. Who has more information? What do they know that you don't? What do you know that they don't?
10. What other options do you have? What other options do they have?

### Cluster C — People + relationship (5 questions)
11. Who is your counterparty as a *person*? (Role, motivations, what do they win/lose if this deal closes vs. dies?)
12. Who's the *real* decision maker on their side? (Often not the person you're emailing.)
13. What's the history with this counterparty? Prior deals? Prior conflicts? Anything that informs their default posture toward you?
14. What's the relationship value going forward? (One-time / repeat / strategic partnership / community-network-embedded?)
15. If they had to describe you behind your back to their partner / lawyer / friend right now — what would they say?

---

## TIER 3 — Deep (Standard + 15 more)

Add these for deals above $250K, equity transactions, real estate, cross-border, or anything with legal exposure.

### Cluster D — Black Swans (5 questions)
16. What does their behavior signal that their *words* haven't said? (Response time changes, who's copied, length of emails, tone shifts.)
17. Is there an internal political situation on their side? (Partner pressure, board pressure, new hire, departure?)
18. Is there a financial pressure on their side? (Cash flow, runway, debt covenant, investor pressure?)
19. Is there an external pressure on their side? (Regulator, competitor, lawsuit, market shift?)
20. What would have to be true on their side for this deal to be *urgent for them* in a way they haven't disclosed?

### Cluster E — Information asymmetry (5 questions)
21. What do you know that they don't know you know? (Hidden information you can deploy.)
22. What do they know that you don't know they know? (Risk — they might surprise you.)
23. What public sources could you check (LinkedIn, court records, press, social, prior deals) before negotiating? (Use WebSearch.)
24. Who in your network has worked with this counterparty before, and what's their read?
25. What documents have you NOT yet read carefully (the actual contract, the actual prior emails, the actual term sheet)?

### Cluster F — Legal + structural (5 questions)
26. What jurisdiction's law governs this deal? Is that jurisdiction favorable to you?
27. What's the worst-case legal scenario, and what does the contract / structure say about it?
28. Are there any non-standard clauses you don't fully understand? (Liquidation prefs, change-of-control, indemnity carve-outs, IP carve-outs, non-competes, etc.)
29. Has a lawyer reviewed this? Should one?
30. Is there a regulatory or licensing dimension you might be missing? (Securities, employment, real estate licensing, tax residency, transfer restrictions.)

## Counterparty Research Playbook

For Tier 2 and Tier 3 deals, do a 15-20 minute web research pass on the counterparty BEFORE negotiating. Use WebSearch and WebFetch.

### Research checklist

1. **LinkedIn profile** — current role, tenure, history. Look for:
   - How long in role (new = less authority + more eagerness)
   - Recent moves (just hired? incentive is to close wins)
   - Previous companies (track record of how they treat counterparties)

2. **Company background**
   - Funding status (recent raise = cash; long since last raise = pressure)
   - Recent news (PR cycle, lawsuits, exec changes, product launches)
   - Glassdoor / employee reviews (signal of internal pressure)
   - Headcount changes (growing = momentum; shrinking = constraint)

3. **Prior deals / track record**
   - Their public partnerships, clients, vendors
   - Press releases naming similar deals (price points, terms if disclosed)
   - SEC filings if public (or for Indonesian PT — OSS / akta records)

4. **Search for negative signals**
   - "[Counterparty] lawsuit" / "[Counterparty] complaint" / "[Counterparty] scam"
   - Reddit / forums where unhappy counterparties post
   - Better Business Bureau (US) / equivalent
   - For real estate / construction: project completion track record

5. **Social signals**
   - Twitter / X — what do they post? What are they angry about?
   - Sub-tweets, public complaints, recent reactivity
   - Industry communities they're in

### Research output format

After the research, write a 6-line briefing:

```
COUNTERPARTY BRIEFING — [Name / Company]

ROLE/AUTHORITY: [What they actually control. Are they the decision maker?]
INCENTIVES: [What do THEY win/lose from this deal?]
PRESSURE SIGNALS: [Any external pressure that bears on the negotiation]
TRACK RECORD: [Pattern with similar counterparties / deals]
LEVERAGE TELLS: [Specific things from research that hint at their BATNA or constraints]
WATCHOUTS: [Red flags — bad reviews, lawsuits, broken-deal pattern]
```

This briefing should never appear in any deliverable to the counterparty. It's internal-only intelligence.

## When to interrupt the user for clarification

You should pause and ask the user a question when:

1. You have a tactical recommendation that depends on a fact you don't know (their BATNA, the counterparty's history, contract language).
2. The deal has cross-domain complexity (legal + financial + relational) and you'd otherwise be guessing.
3. The user's emotional state seems to be driving a move that the wisdom-layer would veto. ("Are you sure you want this deal to close, or is the alternative actually fine?")
4. The deal has hit a fork — two plausible tactical paths with very different downstream consequences. Don't pick on their behalf; surface both.

When asking, batch your questions (max 3-4 per turn) and structure them with the AskUserQuestion tool. Don't dump all 15 in one message.

---

## When you can skip the interrogation

You can fast-track if:

- The deal is a follow-up in a thread you already have full context on
- The user has pasted in the entire email thread + contract + their briefing already
- The deal is under $5K with a known repeat counterparty
- The user explicitly says "skip the interrogation, just help me respond"

But for anything above $25K, even if the user says "just help me respond," at minimum confirm: (1) what's the desired outcome here, (2) what's your BATNA, (3) what's the time pressure on each side. Three questions. Not negotiable.

---

## The interrogation closes when you can answer these 5 yourself:

After running the protocol, you should be able to write:

1. The deal in one sentence.
2. The user's BATNA and the counterparty's BATNA, in one line each.
3. The leverage table — who has more on each of the 5 leverage sources (BATNA, time, information, optionality, reputation/threat).
4. The relationship value going forward (one-time / repeat / strategic).
5. The single Black Swan most likely operating in the background.

If you can't write these 5 lines clearly, you don't have enough context yet. Keep asking.

---

## User calibration

A few common patterns to watch for:

1. **Tendency to assume good faith.** Probe: "Is there any chance their behavior signals bad faith rather than misunderstanding?" Make sure they've tested that hypothesis.
2. **Impatience with open threads.** Probe: "Are you feeling rushed to close this? Is the rush real or is it your own discomfort with the open loop?"
3. **Underestimating community-network leverage.** Probe: "Is this counterparty in any community / network that you also operate in? Does that change your tactical choice?"
4. **Strong on opportunity, sometimes weak on structure.** Probe: "Have you read the actual contract / term sheet / closing docs in full? Or are you operating on the summary version?"
5. **Defaulting to the headline number.** Probe: "Have we considered all the other levers (timing, term, scope, exclusivity, IP, options)? Could any of those move the deal more?"

When any of these patterns surface, name them gently and redirect to a better question.
