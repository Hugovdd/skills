# Playbook: Real Estate — Closing Costs, Handover, and Cross-Border Quirks

Real estate disputes are different from other deals because: (a) there's almost always a written contract with specific clauses governing the issue, (b) physical possession creates leverage neither side wants to give up, (c) closing windows have legal consequences (limitation periods, opt-out windows, recording deadlines), and (d) cross-border real estate — for example Indonesia, which has structural rules that override normal negotiation logic.

This playbook covers the most common dispute types in real estate, with a worked example for Indonesia.

---

## Universal real estate principles

### 1. The contract is the deal.

Whatever was promised verbally during the showing, the negotiation, the offer call — it doesn't matter unless it's in the executed document. Always start by reading the actual contract clauses governing the dispute. Quote them in any back-and-forth. The counterparty arguing against the written contract is at an immediate disadvantage.

### 2. Possession is leverage.

The party holding physical possession has structural leverage. If you don't yet have keys / title / possession and the dispute is over closing terms, the seller has leverage. If you've taken possession and the dispute is over post-close repairs / unpaid items, you have leverage.

**Tactical implication:** Don't surrender possession until material disputes are resolved. Don't agree to take possession with "we'll figure out [X] after close" — that's how you lose leverage.

### 3. Closing is one-way.

Most real estate transactions are difficult to unwind post-closing. The legal mechanisms exist (rescission, breach claims, specific performance) but they're slow and expensive. This means: **disputes resolved BEFORE closing are 10x cheaper than disputes resolved AFTER closing.** Resist all pressure to "close first and resolve later."

### 4. Documentation > memory.

Every promise, repair, credit, scope item, and condition should be written. Email confirmations of verbal calls. Punch lists signed by both parties. Closing statements line-itemed and reconciled. Photos at handover. The party with documentation wins; the party arguing from memory loses.

### 5. The deadlines are real.

Real estate has legal deadlines that you can't talk your way out of: inspection contingency expiry, financing contingency expiry, closing date, recording deadlines, post-close warranty / latent defect periods. Track them. Missing one can void your remedy entirely.

## Common Dispute Types & Tactical Responses

### Dispute Type 1: Who pays which closing costs

**Classic situation:** Contract was signed. At/near closing, one party (often seller) tries to push costs onto the other (often buyer) that the contract assigned to themselves. Examples:
- Transfer tax / stamp duty
- Title insurance
- Notary / PPAT fees
- Brokerage commissions
- Pro-rated property tax
- Utility transfer fees
- HOA / strata transfer fees
- Specific local fees (BPHTB in Indonesia, etc.)

**First move:**
1. Pull the executed contract. Find the closing-cost allocation clause. Read it carefully.
2. Identify EXACTLY which costs are in dispute. Don't argue about "closing costs" abstractly — argue about specific line items.
3. Determine what the contract says about each. If silent, determine local convention.
4. Determine what the counterparty's actual reason is (mistake? bad faith? cash flow problem on their side?).

**Voss + Fisher response template:**

```
Hi [name] —

I want to make sure we're aligned on the closing-cost allocation, because
I want this to close clean.

Per [section X] of the contract dated [date], [specific cost] is allocated
to [seller / buyer]. That's how I've been planning the closing budget.

Help me understand what's changed on your side — is there a specific line
item or amount where we're seeing this differently?

Happy to jump on a call if it's easier.

[Your name]
```

This does: cites contract (objective criterion), surfaces specificity, asks calibrated question to surface their actual reason (Black Swan hunt — is it cash flow? misunderstanding? bad-faith probe?).

**If they push back with "that's not how we usually do it here":**

The "local custom" argument is sometimes legitimate (Indonesian conventions vary), sometimes a manipulation. Counter:

```
"Local custom matters, but the contract we signed addresses this specifically
in [section X]. If there's a local convention that differs, that's something
that should have been in the contract — and going forward, I'd want it in
writing before we adjust. What's your basis for the alternative allocation?"
```

This stays in Fisher's "objective criteria" frame and forces them to either produce documentation or back off.

**Escalation if they refuse to budge:**
- Level 1: Direct named consequence — "If we can't align on the cost allocation by [date], I'll need to either delay closing or have my counsel review the contract for next steps."
- Level 2: Engage local counsel. In Indonesia, your notaris (PPAT) is often the right first call — they know local conventions and have professional incentive to keep the deal compliant.
- Level 3: Threaten / file complaint with relevant body (real estate regulator, professional body, court).

Never escalate to Level 2-3 without (a) clear contract support and (b) willingness to actually deliver.

### Dispute Type 2: Handover defects / property condition

**Classic situation:** Property is supposed to be handed over in a specific condition. At walkthrough or post-possession, there are issues — incomplete work, damage not present at agreement, items removed that should have stayed, broken systems.

**Leverage rule:** **Do NOT close until the handover condition is acceptable, OR until a written, signed escrow holdback covers the remaining work.**

This is the single most violated rule in real estate. Buyer closes "trusting" the seller will finish the punch list. Seller, post-close, slow-rolls or denies. Buyer's leverage is gone. Now the buyer is suing — slow, expensive, often less than the cost of just fixing it themselves.

**Pre-close playbook:**

1. Do the walkthrough with a written punch list — items, expected condition, current condition, remediation required, deadline.
2. Photograph everything (timestamped).
3. Get the seller's countersignature on the punch list.
4. Negotiate: either (a) seller completes work BEFORE closing, or (b) seller escrows a sum (1.5x-2x of estimated cost) that releases on verified completion, or (c) closing credit at the dollar value of the remediation.

**If seller refuses any of these:** that's the negotiation signal. They're trying to off-load the risk onto you. Walk or delay closing.

**Voss response when handover dispute hits:**

```
Hi [name] —

I went through the walkthrough today and there are a few items that don't
match what we agreed to. Specifically:

- [Item 1]: agreed [condition], current [condition]
- [Item 2]: agreed [condition], current [condition]
- [Item 3]: agreed [condition], current [condition]

I want to close on schedule — and I want to make sure these get resolved
cleanly. Two options on my end:

(a) Items 1-3 completed before closing, verified at re-walkthrough on [date].
(b) Closing credit of [$X total = sum of estimated remediation × 1.5] applied
    at close so I can complete them myself.

Which works on your side?

[Your name]
```

This: factual (objective criteria), specific (no abstractions), offers them a structured choice (Voss "false dichotomy" of acceptable options), avoids open-ended fight.

### Dispute Type 3: Title / ownership / encumbrance issues

**These are Tier 1+ legal issues.** Anything related to title (chain of ownership, liens, encumbrances, easements, undisclosed encumbrances, foreign-ownership compliance) should not be self-negotiated. Engage counsel.

**Initial response:** buy time, do not concede or release the issue.

```
"I appreciate you flagging this. Before we proceed further, I need to have
my counsel review the title and encumbrance documents. I'll come back with
substantive next steps once that review is complete."
```

That's it. Send to counsel. Don't draft substantive responses about title issues until you have legal input.

### Dispute Type 4: Earnest money / deposit forfeit

**Classic situation:** Deal falls apart (or is on the edge). Question of who keeps the earnest money / deposit.

**Critical rules:**
- The contract dictates earnest money disposition. Read it.
- If the contract is ambiguous or you can argue contingency exit, document the basis clearly.
- Don't release earnest money to seller until ALL contingencies you're entitled to have been satisfied or fully waived in writing.
- Don't release earnest money to seller as a "good faith" gesture during a dispute — that's leverage gone.

**Voss response when seller demands forfeit:**

```
"I understand you'd like to release the deposit. Before we get there, I want
to walk through which contingencies were satisfied vs. which weren't, per
the contract.

[Specifically: contingency X was satisfied on Y date. Contingency Z was
not satisfied because W happened — that triggers the buyer's right to
terminate under section [N], and per section [N+1], the deposit returns
to buyer.]

If you read it differently, walk me through your interpretation."
```

If the contract clearly favors you, hold ground. If it's ambiguous, propose mediation or shared release per a defined formula.

### Dispute Type 5: Force majeure / contingency delays

When closing date is missed due to "circumstances beyond control" — financing delay, permit delay, force majeure, etc.

**Don't:**
- Agree to extend without consideration (a paid extension, additional deposit, or renegotiated terms)
- Let extensions become indefinite without a hard deadline

**Do:**
- Get extension in writing with a new hard deadline
- Tie extension to specific milestones (financing approval, permit issuance)
- Negotiate consideration for the extension (deposit increase, price adjustment, scope change)

## Cross-Border Example: Indonesia (foreign real estate)

### Foreign ownership structures — KNOW WHICH ONE YOU'RE IN

Before any tactical move on an Indonesian deal, confirm:

1. **What structure** are you in? Hak Pakai, Hak Guna Bangunan, leasehold (sewa), or — danger — a nominee structure?
2. **What's the actual document** — Sertipikat (certified title), or Girik / Letter C (uncertified, much riskier)?
3. **Who's the legal owner of record** — you, your PT PMA, your Indonesian nominee?
4. **What's the term** (for leasehold / Hak Pakai) and what are the renewal mechanics?

If you can't answer these cold, pause. Find out. Then negotiate.

### Common Indonesian dispute patterns

**Pattern A: Nominee suddenly leveraging the structure**

A foreigner buys a villa through an Indonesian nominee. Years in, the nominee (or their family after death/divorce) starts claiming "I own this." Legally, in Indonesia, they DO own it — the side agreements are not enforceable.

- This is a Tier 2 legal situation. Engage Indonesian counsel immediately.
- Do not threaten or escalate publicly — many such cases have ended with the foreigner losing entirely.
- Negotiate a buyout if at all possible. The leverage is largely on the nominee's side.
- Long-term: convert to a structure that doesn't rely on nominees (Hak Pakai, lease, PT PMA where possible).

**Pattern B: Developer slow-rolling completion / handover**

A common pattern in emerging-market developments. Developer takes deposits, starts construction, then stretches handover by 6-12-24 months. Buyer is locked in, can't easily exit.

Tactical moves:
- Read the contract: are there liquidated damages for delay? Penalty clauses?
- Aggregate similar buyers — collective action increases leverage dramatically
- Public visibility — developer reputation matters in tight markets; word travels
- Legal counsel + named consequences — "We'll be filing a complaint with [BKPM / local authority / Pengadilan] if completion hasn't reached [milestone] by [date]"
- Don't release further payments until milestones met in writing
- Bring in a notaris (PPAT) to formally witness the missed milestones — creates a documented record

**Pattern C: Closing costs / fees inflated at handover**

The counterparty (seller, developer, agent) tries to push additional fees onto you at the closing table that weren't in the original agreement. Common items:
- BPHTB (transfer tax, ~5%) — legitimate, but who pays should be in the contract
- Notaris (PPAT) fees — usually buyer
- "Administration" fees — often inflated; question every line item
- "Tax" of various kinds (PPh, PPN if applicable) — verify against actual tax law
- Renovation / improvement payments not in original scope

**Tactical move:**

```
Hi [name] —

Before we close, I want to reconcile the closing-cost breakdown. I have:

[Itemize each cost and what was originally agreed / what the contract specifies]

A few of these are different from what we discussed in [reference to prior
communication]. Specifically:
- [Item]: I had [$X] agreed; current statement shows [$Y]. What's driving
  the difference?
- [Item]: This wasn't part of our original scope. Help me understand why
  it's appearing now.

I'd like to resolve these before signing the akta.

[Your name]
```

Don't sign the akta jual beli (deed of sale) until every line item is reconciled. Once signed, the leverage is gone.

**Pattern D: Notaris / PPAT favoring the seller**

In Indonesia, notaris (PPAT) is theoretically neutral but practically often has stronger relationship with one party (usually the seller, who's a repeat customer). Tactical moves:
- Where possible, propose a notaris you've vetted yourself, OR a neutral one mutually agreed
- Get all documents reviewed by your own lawyer separately, NOT through the closing notaris
- Don't sign anything in a notaris's office under time pressure — request to take documents home for review

### Indonesia — when to engage counsel

For any cross-border RE transaction (Indonesia example):
- **Above $50K equiv**: have an Indonesian commercial lawyer review the contract pre-execution
- **Anything with foreign-ownership structuring**: lawyer mandatory
- **Any dispute that doesn't resolve in 2 emails**: lawyer
- **Anything where the counterparty has lawyered up**: counter-lawyer immediately

Specific lawyer profiles to look for in Indonesia:
- Indonesian licensed (not "consultants" without a bar license)
- Commercial / transactional focus, not just litigation
- Track record with foreign clients
- Speaks English fluently enough for nuanced contract discussion
- Independent of the seller / developer (no shared offices, no shared ownership)

## Example Situation: real estate handover / closing-costs dispute

This pattern fits a closing-cost dispute. Tactical sequencing:

1. **Confirm the contract clause.** Pull the executed agreement. Find the closing-cost allocation clause. Quote it back at the counterparty.
2. **Confirm the specific cost in dispute.** Don't argue abstractly. Get to the line item.
3. **Send a Voss email** with the structure above — cite the contract, surface specificity, ask calibrated question to find their actual reason.
4. **If they push "local custom":** push back with Fisher frame — what's written in the contract governs unless there's a documented basis to deviate.
5. **Hold the line.** Do NOT release closing or sign the akta until this is resolved in writing.
6. **If they don't move in 48-72 hours:** Level 2 escalation — engage your notaris (or a different one if the current one is too aligned with seller) to formally document the dispute. Then named consequence — "If not resolved by [date], I'll need to delay closing and have my counsel review."
7. **If they STILL don't move:** Lawyer up. The cost of a local commercial lawyer to send a letter is typically < $500. The cost of eating an unfair closing cost can be much higher. Math is asymmetric.

## Universal sanity check

Before any meaningful RE move, ask the user:

1. **Have you read the actual signed contract?** Not summarized — read.
2. **What's your BATNA?** If this deal dies, what's the realistic alternative?
3. **What's possession status?** Are you currently leveraged or unleveraged by possession?
4. **What deadlines are running?** Contingency, closing, recording, post-close warranty?
5. **Who has you covered legally?** If the answer is "no one" and the deal is > $50K, fix that before negotiating.

If any of these are "I don't know" — fix that first. Don't negotiate from ignorance on a multi-six-figure deal.
