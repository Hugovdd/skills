# Playbook: Email Thread Negotiation

This is the user's PRIMARY use case. Most of their deals run through email — clients, vendors, partners, real estate counterparties, prospective hires. Email negotiation is its own discipline: the medium is lossy (no tone, no body language), every word becomes evidence, and the asynchronous cycle changes which tactics work.

This playbook is the operational guide for: "They just sent me this email. What do I send back?"

---

## The Pre-Reply Protocol (do this BEFORE drafting)

When the user pastes in a counterparty email, do this analysis FIRST. Don't jump to drafting.

### Step 1 — Decode their email (Voss read)

Read it slowly. Note:

- **Literal content**: what specifically did they say?
- **Subtext**: what are they implying but not stating?
- **Absences**: what did they NOT say that you'd expect from this kind of counterparty?
- **Emotion driving the message**: anxiety, anger, triumph, fear, frustration, professional distance?
- **Tactical structure**: are they running a known tactic (ultimatum, anchor, foot-in-the-door, false scarcity, deadline pressure)?
- **Information signals**: response time relative to baseline? Length relative to baseline? Cc'd recipients new vs. expected? Sign-off changed?
- **What their BATNA looks like from your read of this email**

Write a 3-5 line "situation read" before doing anything else. This goes at the top of the response template.

### Step 2 — Confirm context with the user

Even if the user has shared the email, you usually need:

- Their full reply chain (not just the last message)
- The underlying contract / term sheet / agreement (if any)
- The user's BATNA in 1 sentence
- Their desired outcome ("what does good look like here?")
- Anything about the counterparty they haven't shared but you need (history, internal pressure, your prior moves)

Ask using AskUserQuestion if any of these are missing. Don't proceed naked.

### Step 3 — Decide the school

Based on `philosophy.md`:
- Default: **Voss tactical empathy** layered over **Fisher's interest-first frame**
- Escalate to **Trump leverage** only if (a) one-shot relationship, (b) leverage asymmetrically in the user's favor, (c) counterparty has acted in bad faith
- If equity / valuation / complex structural: weight **Fisher** heavier (more levers)
- If purely interpersonal / relational: weight **Voss** heavier (more empathy)
- If you sense the user is emotionally committed to a deal that shouldn't happen: pull in **wisdom-layer** before tactics

### Step 4 — Build the leverage map

Standard format (see SKILL.md). Force articulation. Don't skip.

### Step 5 — Trigger legal layer if warranted

Run the escalation matrix from `legal-layer.md`. If Tier 1+ triggers, do the web research now.

### Step 6 — Draft the response

Now and only now, draft.

## The Voss Email Template (your default skeleton)

For most situations, structure the reply like this:

```
[Greeting — appropriate to the relationship. No "I hope this finds you well" filler.]

[Accusation Audit — ONE sentence. "I know this is going to sound [X], but..."]

[Label or Acknowledgment — ONE sentence. "It seems like [their emotion/position]..." or "I understand that [their constraint]..."]

[Restatement / Summary — show you actually understood what they said. ONE-TWO sentences.]

[Your substantive move — the actual position, ask, or counter. Concise. With basis (objective criteria) if appropriate.]

[Calibrated Question — forces them to engage. "How can we...?" or "What about this is...?"]

[Close — short. No apology. No "let me know if this works!!" Sign off with confidence.]
```

**Length target:** Most high-leverage emails are 80-200 words. If yours is 500 words, you're over-explaining. If it's 30 words, you might be missing the empathy work.

## Move Library — When To Use Which Email Tactic

### Move 1 — The Reframe

When the counterparty is locked on a position, reframe what you're negotiating about.

```
Example:
THEM: "We can't go above $40K. That's our final number."

YOU:
"Got it on the $40K ceiling. Before we lock on price, I want to make sure
we've optimized the structure. There are 3-4 other levers that affect the
deal economics for both of us — term length, scope, exclusivity, and
payment timing.

If we extended the term by 6 months, would that free up budget on the
headline? Or alternatively, if I narrowed scope to [X], we could land at
your $40K without me eating margin."
```

Voss + Fisher in one move: acknowledged their position, didn't fight it, expanded the pie.

### Move 2 — The Calibrated "No"

When they ask for something unreasonable, don't say "no" directly.

```
THEM: "We need a 30-day refund window if results don't meet expectations."

YOU:
"I hear you — 'results don't meet expectations' is doing a lot of work
in that sentence. Help me understand what 'meeting expectations' would
look like to you, specifically. If we can define the success criteria
together, I'm happy to discuss a refund mechanism tied to those specific
outcomes. Where I get nervous is on an open-ended discretion clause —
how am I supposed to invest in delivery if 'didn't meet expectations'
is whatever you decide on day 28?"
```

The calibrated question puts the problem back on them while opening the door to a structured version of what they want.

### Move 3 — The Anchor Drop

When you need to set the field early. Use sparingly — only when your information is strong.

```
YOU (first move):
"Before we get too far — based on [comp / market data / prior precedent],
the range I'd expect on this kind of deal is $X-$Y. I'm targeting $Z,
which sits at the [low/mid/upper] end of that range because [reason].
Curious where you're at."
```

Anchor + objective criterion + invite. The anchor is defended with data, not pulled out of thin air. Then a calibrated extraction of their range.

### Move 4 — The Time Reframe

When they're using deadline pressure on you.

```
THEM: "We need a decision by end of day Friday."

YOU:
"I hear the urgency. Help me understand what's driving the Friday
deadline — is there an external trigger, or is that more of an internal
target? I want to make sure we land this right. A rushed yes on terms
I haven't fully worked through is worse for both of us than an extra
week of careful thinking."
```

Reframes their pressure as a shared problem, surfaces the *real* deadline (Black Swan: is it external or internal?), and reasserts your willingness to delay without sounding like you're stalling.

### Move 5 — The "What's Behind That?"

When their position seems arbitrary or extreme.

```
THEM: "We need 50% equity to do this deal."

YOU:
"50% is a big number. What's driving that — is it about voting control,
upside protection, or something else entirely? I want to make sure I
understand the underlying concern, because the structure to address it
might not be a 50% equity stake."
```

Surfaces interest behind position. Opens trade space.

### Move 6 — The Soft Walk

When you need to remind them you have a BATNA without threatening.

```
"I want this deal to work, and I think there's a version of it that's
good for both of us. The version on the table right now isn't that
version for me, and I want to be honest about that rather than agree
to something I can't deliver well. What's the version that gets you
to where you need to be while still making sense on my side?"
```

Honest, non-aggressive, but the implicit message is "I'm willing to walk." Trump-flavor leverage, Voss-style delivery.

### Move 7 — The Hard Walk (use rarely)

When you're actually willing to kill the deal and you want them to know.

```
"I appreciate you working through this. I don't think we're going to
land in the same place on this deal. I'm going to step back and explore
other options on my end. If something changes that brings us closer
together, my door's open."
```

Brief. No anger. No insult. No "final offer of $X" — that's just another negotiation. This is the actual exit. Send it when you're ready to mean it.

(After a hard walk, ~30% of the time the counterparty comes back with a real concession within 48-72 hours. That's the point. But you have to be willing to mean it for it to work.)

### Move 8 — The Accusation Audit Open

When the email might land defensively, pre-empt.

```
"I want to come at this directly. I know my push on [X] might look
like I'm trying to get out of what we agreed to. I'm not — what I am
trying to do is make sure the deal on paper matches the deal we both
intended. Specifically, [Y] doesn't read the way I understood it in
our last conversation."
```

Disarms their likely defensive read before they can build it.

### Move 9 — The Specific Counter

When you need to offer a specific alternative.

```
"Here's what I can do: [specific number / structure]. I'm landing there
because [reason — basis, math, comparable deal]. That said, if [X]
is more important to you than [Y], we can flex on that — what's the
priority on your side?"
```

Specific number, defended by reason, with a built-in option for them to redirect.

### Move 10 — The Pause / "Sleeping On It"

When their email is provocative and you'd write something you'd regret.

```
[Don't reply same day. Wait 24-48 hours. Then:]

"Hey [name] — I've been thinking on your last message and I want to come
back to you tomorrow with a real response, not a quick one. Talk soon."
```

Short. Buys time without conceding. Signals you're taking it seriously. Costs you nothing.

## Email Anti-Patterns (don't send any of these)

### 1. The "I just want to be fair" gambit

Saying "I just want this to be fair" pre-concedes that fairness is up for negotiation. It also signals that you might capitulate. Replace with: state your position with a basis. The basis is the fairness argument.

### 2. The over-explanation

Long emails justifying your position. The longer your justification, the weaker you sound. Voss: "The best negotiators are comfortable with silence." Shorter is stronger.

### 3. The triple-question

Three questions in a row dilutes each one. Pick ONE calibrated question per email. Make them answer it.

### 4. The "let me know if this works!!"

Punctuation pleads. Trail off into space. Let them respond.

### 5. The unforced concession

Offering a discount, an extension, or a scope expansion they didn't ask for. Every concession you make should be in response to an ask, and every concession should get something in return.

### 6. The CC escalation (when YOU shouldn't be the one escalating)

Don't cc their boss / lawyer / partner unless you've decided to escalate. CC'ing changes the dynamic. Single-track when you can.

### 7. The "per my last email"

Passive-aggressive. Burns relationship. Costs more than it gains. Either escalate properly or don't.

### 8. The Friday-afternoon send

Sending a heavy email at 4pm Friday means it sits all weekend amplifying their stress (and yours). Send Tuesday-Thursday morning when possible.

### 9. The drunk-tap reply

Never reply when angry, drunk, hungry, exhausted, or in transit. Sleep on it. Always. (Many people are wired to want closure, which makes the drunk-tap reply tempting. Resist.)

### 10. The "to be honest"

Filler that signals you've been less than honest before. Cut.

## Email Calibration — Tone Spectrum

Match tone to deal stage and counterparty energy. Here's the spectrum from softest to hardest:

| Level | Tone | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **L1 — Warm collaborative** | "Hey, working through this with you..." | Long relationship, low-stakes back-and-forth |
| **L2 — Cordial professional** | "Hi [name], following up on our conversation..." | New relationship, normal business deal |
| **L3 — Direct, neutral** | "[name] — here's where I'm at..." | Stakes rising, you want to be heard clearly |
| **L4 — Crisp, named-consequences** | "I want to be clear about how this needs to land..." | Counterparty starting to take advantage |
| **L5 — Formal, lawyered tone** | "Per the agreement dated [X], section [Y] provides..." | Real dispute mode, evidence-building |
| **L6 — Counsel-drafted or counsel-cc'd** | Drafted by a lawyer | Tier 2 legal escalation |

**Default for the user: L2 with strategic L3 moves.** Escalate up the ladder only when needed, and ONLY one level at a time. Skipping levels makes you look unstable.

**Voice notes:** the user's voice is direct, casual-confident, no fluff. Don't write them scripts that sound corporate. "Hey [name]" beats "Dear [name]" 90% of the time for them. Contractions are fine. Sentence fragments are fine when used deliberately for emphasis.

## Branch Planning — "If they say X, I say Y"

Before sending, anticipate their likely 2-3 replies and have your move prepared. Include this in the deliverable to the user.

```
IF THEY: agree to your terms
THEN YOU: confirm in writing, ask about next steps and timeline

IF THEY: counter with a slightly worse position
THEN YOU: [specific concession + ask] — see Move 9 (specific counter)

IF THEY: come back hostile / threatening
THEN YOU: pause 24h, then Move 6 (soft walk) — see escalation ladder

IF THEY: ghost / don't respond in 3+ business days
THEN YOU: short bump after 4 days, then 7 days, then assume dead
```

The branch plan isn't a script — it's preparation. The user should know what their next move is BEFORE their reply lands. That preserves cool decision-making.

## Follow-Up Hygiene

When you DON'T get a reply, the follow-up sequence:

- **Day 0:** Send the message
- **Day +3-4 business days:** First short bump. ("Wanted to make sure this didn't get buried — open to your thoughts.")
- **Day +8-10:** Second bump, slightly different angle. ("Don't want to assume — should I take the silence as a no? Happy to close this out either way.")
- **Day +14:** Assume dead. Move on. Update your BATNA.

Rules:
- Each follow-up SHORTER than the last
- Never "checking in" — that signals weakness
- Always give them a clean off-ramp ("happy to close this out either way") — keeps you in control of the optionality

## The Real Test: would you be okay if this email were read aloud in court?

Final check before sending any meaningful negotiation email. Read it back through this lens:

- Tone: would a judge read this as professional and fair, or as hot/petty/threatening?
- Specificity: are the facts you've asserted defensible?
- Acknowledgments: have you acknowledged any wrongdoing you didn't actually commit?
- Promises: are you making promises you might walk back?
- Insults: any? Remove them.
- Threats: any? Make sure each is something you'd actually deliver.

If anything fails this test, rewrite. Email is evidence. Always.

## User email patterns to deploy / avoid

### Patterns that work well for the user
- **Direct openings**: "Hey [name] — I want to be direct here." Sets tone, owns the conversation.
- **Casual-confident sign-off**: `[Your name]` (no "Best," "Warm regards," etc. for most relationships). Relaxed-but-serious.
- **Specific numbers**: "$3,847" beats "$4K" for them. Specific implies analysis, fits their voice.
- **Off-ramps via call**: "Happy to jump on a call if it's easier." Always include for medium-stakes emails; phone is their secret weapon.

### Patterns to avoid for the user
- Corporate-sounding language ("synergies," "alignment," "circle back") — not their voice, and reads inauthentic
- Excessive politeness/hedging ("I was just wondering if perhaps..." — kill it)
- Length over substance — the user's voice is tight
- Em-dash overuse (a real AI tell) — use sparingly, prefer periods or commas
- Rule-of-three constructions ("decisive, deliberate, and determined") — AI tell, kill it
- "I hope this finds you well" garbage — never

### When to invoke humanizer
After drafting, run a quick humanizer pass. Common AI tells to remove:
- "delve," "unlock," "navigate," "tapestry," "leverage" (as verb), "ensure"
- Em-dashes everywhere
- Title-case headings inside emails
- Rule of three
- Superficial -ing analyses
- Vague attributions

If the draft fails the humanizer pass, rewrite tighter.

## The deliverable the user gets back

For an in-deal email response, the deliverable format:

```
## SITUATION READ
[Your Voss-style decode of their last message]

## LEVERAGE MAP
[Filled out per philosophy.md]

## STRATEGY (3-5 bullets)
[Which school dominant, why, what concession path, what anchor, what calibrated question]

## DRAFT RESPONSE
[The actual email — polished, voice-matched, ready to send]

## WHY EACH MOVE (annotated)
[For each meaningful move: "This sentence does X because..."]

## RED FLAGS / LEGAL NOTES
[Anything from legal-layer.md]

## IF THEY: ___ / THEN YOU: ___
[2-3 likely branches with the user's prepared next move]

## SEND-CHECK
- [ ] Read back through "would I be okay if this were read in court?"
- [ ] Humanizer pass: no em-dashes, no "delve/unlock/navigate," no AI tells
- [ ] Short enough? (under 200 words target)
- [ ] Calibrated question included? (forces engagement)
- [ ] No unforced concessions?
```

Always include the SEND-CHECK at the bottom. It's the last gate before sending.
