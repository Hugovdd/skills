# Playbook: Post-Mortem Analysis

After a deal closes (win, loss, or stalemate), the negotiator who learns from it compounds. The one who doesn't repeats the same mistake. This playbook is the structured after-action review.

Dalio's principle: **Pain + Reflection = Progress.** The deals that hurt are the ones with the most to teach. The deals that went well still have lessons hidden in them — you just have to dig.

---

## When to run a post-mortem

- **Every deal above $25K of value**, regardless of outcome
- **Every deal that hurt** — closed badly, fell apart, ended with bad blood
- **Every deal that surprised you** — even if it went well, if it didn't go how you expected, there's a lesson
- **Periodically (quarterly?)** — a portfolio review of recent deals as a batch

Aim for the post-mortem within 1-2 weeks of close. Long enough that emotion has cooled. Short enough that memory is fresh.

---

## The Post-Mortem Template

Use this structure. Be honest. The point of this exercise is private learning, not narrative-building.

```markdown
# Post-Mortem: [Deal name]

**Date of close (or death):** [date]
**Type:** [contract / equity / real estate / settlement / etc.]
**Counterparty:** [name]
**Headline outcome:** [closed at $X / fell apart / settled at $Y]

---

## 1. Original goal vs. outcome

**What did I want going in?** [Best case / acceptable case / walk-away point]
**What did I get?** [Headline result, all material terms]
**How does the outcome compare to BATNA?** [Better / equal / worse than what I'd have done if no deal]

(Critical question: would my life be meaningfully different if this had gone to BATNA instead? Often the answer is "barely.")

---

## 2. Leverage map — what I had vs. what I thought I had

**Going in, I believed my leverage was:** [score 1-5 on BATNA, time, info, optionality, reputation]
**Actual leverage in retrospect:** [score 1-5 on each]
**Where was I wrong?**

(Most common bias: overstating your own leverage, understating theirs.)

---

## 3. Black Swans — what did I miss?

**What information surfaced DURING or AFTER the deal that, if I'd known going in, would have changed how I negotiated?**

Examples:
- Their actual deadline
- Their actual BATNA
- A competitor's offer to them
- Personal / financial / political pressure on their side
- A regulatory or legal threat they hadn't disclosed
- Their relationship with someone in your network

**For each Black Swan: what could I have done to find this BEFORE the deal closed?**

---

## 4. Tactical analysis — what worked and what didn't

| Move | School / Source | Worked? | What did it produce? |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Move 1] | [Voss/Trump/Fisher/intuition] | Y/N | [Specific outcome] |
| [Move 2] | ... | ... | ... |

**Best move (most leverage extracted):** ___
**Worst move (left value on table / damaged position):** ___

---

## 5. Counterparty analysis

**Their tactical pattern:** [What did they do? Aggressive opener? Slow-roll? Multiple deadlines? Surprise demands at close?]
**Their behavior at key moments:** [What did their email length / response time / tone signal at the inflection points?]
**Their probable interests in retrospect:** [What were they actually trying to optimize?]
**Would I want to negotiate with this person again?** [Yes / no / depends on terms]

---

## 6. Emotional state log

**Where was I emotionally during the deal?** [Honest assessment]
**Did emotion cause any specific moves I regret?** [Anger send? Capitulation under fatigue? Optimism bias on terms?]
**What would I do differently to manage state?** [Cool-off periods, advisor before signing, etc.]

---

## 7. Structural / contract review (if applicable)

**Are there terms in the executed agreement I now regret?** [List them]
**Are there terms I should have insisted on but didn't?** [List them]
**Were any terms ambiguous in ways I'll have to manage going forward?** [Flag them; calendar follow-up]

---

## 8. Process review

**What did I do to prepare?** [BATNA articulation, leverage map, counterparty research, advisor involvement]
**What should I have done that I didn't?** [Honest answer]
**How long did the deal take? Was that the right amount of time?** [Too fast? Too slow?]
**Did I have the right people in the loop?** [Lawyer, accountant, advisor, partner?]

---

## 9. The Munger Inversion check

**5 years from now, will I regret any aspect of this deal?**
**What would make me regret it?**
**Is there anything I should set up NOW to prevent that future regret?** (Calendar reminders, monitoring, relationships to maintain, options to preserve.)

---

## 10. Lessons — what goes into the playbook

**Three concrete lessons:**
1. [Specific, actionable lesson]
2. [Specific, actionable lesson]
3. [Specific, actionable lesson]

**Update to standing principles:** [Anything that should be added to the user's pre-deal principles list?]

**Save these lessons:** [Where will they live so I'll see them next time? Memory file, principle doc, notes?]
```

## Red Flags to Surface in a Post-Mortem

Watch for these patterns. If they're present, the lesson is sharp:

### "I closed because I was tired"

You agreed to terms not because they were right but because the negotiation was draining. Future principle: schedule more time, take cool-off breaks, ask "would I sign this if I were fresh?"

### "I overestimated my leverage"

You went in thinking you had cards you didn't. Counterparty knew. They squeezed. Future principle: cold-read your leverage map, ideally with someone who has no skin in the deal.

### "I underestimated their leverage"

You missed something they had — a regulatory threat over you, a relationship with someone in your network, a Black Swan you didn't hunt. Future principle: invest more in counterparty research before negotiating.

### "I let urgency override judgment"

They imposed (or you accepted) a deadline that pressured a bad decision. Future principle: artificial urgency is real leverage, and the response is patience.

### "I anchored too low"

You opened with a number that felt fair, and ended up below it. Future principle: anchor with comp-supported numbers above your target, defend with data.

### "I didn't get it in writing"

Verbal promises during the deal didn't make it into the final document. Now they're disputed. Future principle: written confirmation of every meaningful agreement, in real time.

### "I missed a clause that's costing me"

A term you didn't focus on during redline is now creating cost or constraint. Future principle: which clauses get my attention is itself a decision; the playbook has the 80/20 priority list.

### "I trusted them more than I should have"

Verbal assurances during the deal didn't match how they behaved post-close. Future principle: trust is a wonderful preference, not a substitute for structure.

### "The relationship cost me leverage"

You softened your moves to "preserve the relationship" and lost value. The relationship continued anyway (or didn't). Future principle: the relationship is sometimes worth the give. Sometimes it's not. Name the cost explicitly when making the trade.

### "I should have walked"

You closed a deal that, in retrospect, would have been better as no-deal. Future principle: the BATNA pre-check + Buffett's "say no to almost everything" should be on a card you read before signing.

## The Portfolio Review (Quarterly)

Once a quarter, run a meta-post-mortem across all recent deals:

### Questions to ask

1. **Patterns in counterparty selection**: am I consistently negotiating with the same problematic type? (e.g., always with cash-strapped vendors who price-renegotiate at close)
2. **Patterns in deal type**: are there deal categories I should stop taking entirely?
3. **Patterns in my own tactical defaults**: am I always opening too low? Always conceding on the same lever?
4. **Patterns in advisor / counsel usage**: am I lawyering up when I should, or only after problems hit?
5. **Patterns in deal portfolio mix**: is the mix of deals I'm taking compounding toward my goals or distracting from them?

### The leverage-improving questions

1. What could I have done in the last quarter to improve my BATNA across multiple deals at once? (Generating better alternatives, building reputation, adding skills, deepening relationships.)
2. What information sources / research patterns have produced the best Black Swan finds?
3. Which tactics from my playbook am I consistently neglecting? (Most negotiators have favorite moves and ignore the rest of the toolkit.)
4. Where am I leaving leverage on the table by default?

### The decision-rule update

After portfolio review, update your standing decision rules. Examples you might land on:
- "Don't sign anything in the same week I first see it" (slow down)
- "Cap personal time on any deal at X hours per week" (don't let negotiation eat life)
- "Always have a non-counterparty advisor read the contract before signing" (second pair of eyes)
- "For foreign-market deals, always involve [specific lawyer] before terms lock in" (don't repeat mistakes)
- "If a deal hasn't progressed materially in 2 weeks, walk or escalate" (don't let stale deals drain bandwidth)

## Storing post-mortems

These are valuable. They compound. Don't just write them and forget.

Recommended:
- Save to the user's notes or a dedicated deals log
- Tag with deal type, counterparty type, and outcome
- Link to relevant other notes (counterparty, contract, related deals)
- Surface in MEMORY.md if a lesson is generalizable across deals
- Review the post-mortems folder before any new deal of similar type

The point of compounding judgment is that the lessons SHOW UP at decision time. If they're buried in a folder you never reread, they don't compound. Build the surfacing into your process.

---

## When NOT to post-mortem

Skip the post-mortem for:
- Trivial / sub-$5K deals
- Routine repeat deals with no surprises
- Active emotional rawness (wait 1-2 weeks; the post-mortem is for cold analysis, not for venting)
- Cases where the lesson is so obvious you don't need a structured review

But err on the side of doing them. The cost is 20 minutes. The benefit compounds.

---

## A note on win-bias

When you win, the post-mortem is harder to take seriously. "It worked, what's there to learn?" Resist this. Wins often hide:
- Things you did right but couldn't have repeated knowingly
- Things that went right despite (not because of) your moves
- Counterparty errors that you got the benefit of, which you shouldn't count on next time
- Survivor bias — the "moves that worked" might also be the moves that almost blew up other deals

Run the post-mortem on wins. The lessons there are real, even if quieter.

## Quick template (lightweight version)

For deals where the full template is overkill, the 5-line post-mortem:

```
Deal: [name]
Outcome: [headline result]
Best move I made: ___
Biggest mistake: ___
What I'd do differently: ___
Lesson to remember: ___
```

5 lines. 5 minutes. Surfaces 80% of the value.
