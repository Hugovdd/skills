# Playbook: Equity, Valuation, and Term Sheets

Equity negotiations are different. They're not about a single number — they're about a structure that compounds for years. A "great" valuation with bad terms can be worse than a "bad" valuation with clean terms. Most founder mistakes in equity deals are made by negotiating only the headline (valuation, cap, $ amount) while ignoring the structure (preferences, vesting, control, anti-dilution).

This playbook covers: term-sheet review, valuation negotiation, founder splits, hiring with equity, advisor equity, acquisition / exit deals, and the most common founder traps.

---

## Universal equity principles

### 1. Valuation is one of ~15 levers.

The lever everyone fights over is valuation (or cap, for SAFEs/notes). The 14 others (liquidation pref, participation, anti-dilution, vesting, board, protective provisions, drag, tag, ROFR, pro-rata, info rights, expense reimbursement, MFN, no-shop) are often where the actual economic outcomes diverge by millions of dollars over time.

**Discipline:** never fight valuation in isolation. Always model the deal across ALL major levers.

### 2. Headline economics ≠ real economics.

A $10M valuation with a 2x participating liquidation preference and full ratchet anti-dilution can leave the founder with substantially less in an exit than a $7M valuation with 1x non-participating and weighted-average anti-dilution. Always run the waterfall under multiple exit scenarios before signing.

### 3. Control matters as much as cash.

Board seats, protective provisions, founder removal rights, and major-decision veto rights are control levers. A founder who keeps 60% of equity but loses board control has effectively lost the company. Don't optimize cash and lose control.

### 4. Time matters — vesting, acceleration, term length.

Equity that vests over 4 years with a 1-year cliff is a 4-year commitment. Acceleration triggers (single trigger vs. double trigger on change-of-control) determine what happens if you're forced out. Term length on notes / SAFEs determines refinance risk. Get these right.

### 5. Tax structure matters as much as legal structure.

83(b) elections, qualified small business stock (QSBS — Section 1202 in US), capital gains treatment, holding periods — these affect after-tax proceeds dramatically. Always involve a tax CPA familiar with founder economics.

### 6. The term sheet is the deal.

The "final" docs (SPA, charter, voting agreement, IRA, ROFR/co-sale) follow the term sheet. If something is wrong in the term sheet, it's likely going into final docs. Spend negotiation energy on the term sheet, not on cleaning up final docs.

## Term Sheet Anatomy — The 15 Levers

For each, the founder-friendly position, the typical "fair" middle, and the toxic version to refuse.

### 1. Valuation (pre-money vs. post-money)

- **Pre-money valuation**: what the company is worth before the investment
- **Post-money valuation**: pre-money + new investment

A $4M pre / $1M investment / $5M post means investors get 20% (1/5). A $4M post / $1M investment / $3M pre means investors get 25% (1/4). Crucial to specify which.

For SAFEs and notes: "post-money SAFE" (YC's 2018 version) is more investor-favorable than pre-money. Founders should usually prefer pre-money on SAFEs.

### 2. Liquidation Preference

What investors get back on exit BEFORE founders/common.

- **Founder-friendly**: 1x non-participating (the default and market-standard)
- **Typical**: 1x non-participating
- **Toxic**: 2x+ participating with no cap

"Participating preferred" means investors get their preference AND their pro-rata share of remaining proceeds. "Non-participating" means they choose: either preference OR pro-rata (whichever is more). Always push for non-participating.

### 3. Anti-Dilution

What happens to investor ownership if the company raises a future round at a lower valuation (down round).

- **Founder-friendly**: Broad-based weighted average
- **Acceptable**: Weighted average
- **Toxic**: Full ratchet (resets the investor's price to the new low price — massively dilutes founders)

Almost always push for broad-based weighted average. Full ratchet is toxic except in rare cases (very late-stage, very strategic).

### 4. Pro-Rata Rights

Investor's right to participate in future rounds to maintain their ownership %.

- Standard for major investors, usually fine. Watch for: pro-rata rights for too many investors (cap-table clutter) or "super pro-rata" (more than their existing %).

### 5. Pay-to-Play

If an investor doesn't participate in a future round, their preferred shares convert to common.

- **Good for company / founders**: forces investors to keep supporting
- **Investors usually push back**, but it's a legitimate company-friendly term to push for

### 6. Drag-Along Rights

Forces minority investors to sell if a majority approves a sale.

- Necessary for clean exits. The question is the threshold (majority of preferred, majority of common, board approval).
- Push for: drag triggered by reasonable thresholds, not by single early investor

### 7. Tag-Along Rights

If founder sells, minority investors can sell pro-rata in the same transaction.

- Standard. Be aware that this can constrain founder liquidity events.

### 8. Vesting

Schedule under which founder/employee equity vests.

- **Standard**: 4 years total, 1-year cliff, monthly thereafter
- **Acceleration on change of control**:
  - **Single trigger**: vests immediately on CoC. Founder-friendly but investors hate it.
  - **Double trigger**: vests on CoC + termination within X months. Standard.

- **Founder vesting on existing shares (reverse vesting)**: required by many investors. Negotiate cliff length.

### 9. Board Composition

Who controls major decisions.

- **Early stage (seed)**: typical is 2 founders + 1 investor, or 1 founder + 1 investor + 1 independent
- **Series A**: typical is 2-1-2 (founders / investors / independents) or 2-2-1
- **Don't lose board control** in early rounds. Once you do, you don't get it back.

### 10. Protective Provisions

What requires investor consent to do.

- **Reasonable**: changing certificate of incorp, issuing senior securities, sale of company, taking on excessive debt
- **Watch out**: budget approval, executive hiring/firing, opening new business lines (too restrictive)

Push to limit protective provisions to truly major actions.

### 11. Right of First Refusal (ROFR) on Founder Transfers

Investor (and company) get first right to buy if founder wants to sell their shares.

- Standard. Sometimes watch the mechanics (timing windows, approval rights).

### 12. Information Rights / Inspection Rights

What financial/operational info investors get and when.

- Major investors: monthly/quarterly financials, annual budget, audit rights at their expense
- Limit broad inspection rights to avoid future-deal friction

### 13. No-Shop / Exclusivity

Founder commits not to talk to other investors for X period during deal close.

- **Standard**: 30-60 days
- **Toxic**: 90+ days, or no end date

This is a real concession — it removes optionality. Negotiate hard.

### 14. MFN (Most Favored Nation)

If a future deal has better terms, this investor gets those terms too.

- Common in SAFEs and notes. Generally fine for early rounds. Becomes complex if you have a lot of MFN holders.

### 15. Founder Removal / Replacement

Conditions under which the founder can be removed.

- **Founder-friendly**: only for cause (defined narrowly)
- **Investor-friendly**: board can remove without cause
- Negotiate "cause" definition carefully

## Term Sheet Review Protocol

When the user (or a counterparty) puts a term sheet on the table:

### Step 1 — Run the 15-lever audit

Go through each lever above. For each:
- What does the term sheet say?
- What's the standard / fair range?
- Is this term sheet inside or outside that range?
- What's the dollar impact if you're forced into the offered position?

### Step 2 — Waterfall the exit scenarios

Model the founder's take-home under 4 scenarios:
- **Low exit** (1x the post-money valuation)
- **Median exit** (3x)
- **High exit** (10x)
- **Down-side / acqui-hire** (50% of post-money)

For each, calculate founder proceeds AFTER liquidation preferences, participation, and any anti-dilution effects.

The shocking finding from most term-sheet waterfalls: in median-exit scenarios, the LIQUIDATION PREFERENCE (especially 2x participating) costs founders more than the valuation hit they were fighting against.

### Step 3 — Identify the 3 biggest gives

You won't push back on every term. Pick the 3 that matter most based on your waterfall. Common big-three to push:
1. Liquidation preference structure (1x non-participating)
2. Anti-dilution mechanism (broad-based weighted average)
3. Board composition (don't lose control)

For each, have your "ask" ready, your "fallback" ready, and your "walk" position ready.

### Step 4 — Negotiate the term sheet, not the docs

Most leverage is during term sheet negotiation. Once signed, the lawyered docs follow. Renegotiating after the term sheet is signed is much harder.

## Valuation Negotiation Tactics

### Anchor with comps

Valuations are weak on absolute logic, strong on relative logic. "Companies at our stage / metrics / market raised at $X-$Y" beats "we're worth $Z." Use:
- Public market comps (revenue multiples, growth rates)
- Recent private comps (similar stage, similar market)
- Industry benchmarks (e.g., SaaS revenue multiples at given growth/retention rates)

Sources: PitchBook (paid), Crunchbase, CB Insights, SaaS Capital Index, public filings, Y Combinator data, founder communities.

### Frame to the right metric

If your headline metrics are weak, anchor on a stronger metric. "Revenue isn't the right metric — we're a marketplace, look at GMV / take rate / cohort retention." Choose your anchor metric carefully.

### Use the "what would have to be true" question

When investor lowballs:

```
"You're proposing $X. Help me understand the model — what would have to
be true about [revenue trajectory / market size / unit economics] for that
valuation to be supported? I want to make sure we're working from the
same assumptions."
```

Forces them to justify with logic, exposes weak assumptions.

### The dual-anchor

Open with your number AND a basis. Don't just say "$10M post." Say "$10M post — that's [N]x ARR, in line with [peer benchmarks], and lower than [peer deal] at similar metrics."

### Concessions traded across levers

If they insist on a lower valuation, trade for cleaner structure (1x non-part, broad-based anti-dilution, board control). The waterfall often makes the structural concession more valuable than the valuation concession.

### Multiple offers as leverage

Best valuation leverage: a competing term sheet. Even a weaker competing term sheet from a less-preferred investor changes the negotiation. Engineer for this — run the process with multiple investors in parallel.

## Founder Splits (When You're the One Co-Founding)

If the user is co-founding (or splitting equity with co-founders), the key tactical points:

### Default 50/50 is usually wrong

The temptation to "be fair" with 50/50 splits often produces deadlock when disagreements happen. Common alternatives:
- 51/49 with one founder as "tiebreaker" CEO
- Tiered splits based on contributions (time, capital, idea, relationships, ongoing operating commitment)
- Vesting differential — earlier or more-committed founder vests faster

### Use a contribution worksheet

Score each founder on:
- Idea origination
- Domain expertise
- Capital contribution
- Time commitment (full-time vs. part-time vs. advisory)
- Network / customer relationships
- Specific risk taken (quit job? mortgaged house? cash invested?)
- Ongoing operating role (CEO vs. CFO vs. CTO vs. advisor-equity)

Score each, weight by importance to the venture, derive split.

### Vesting is non-negotiable

Even between best friends co-founding. 4-year vest, 1-year cliff. Acceleration triggers spelled out. The reason: in 1-2 years, one founder may leave (for reasons no one anticipates). Without vesting, they walk with their full equity. With vesting, the company recovers unvested shares for the people still building it.

### Buyback rights / departure clauses

What happens if a co-founder leaves? Get it in writing:
- Vested shares: retained (with possible company ROFR)
- Unvested shares: returned to the option pool
- Bad-leaver clauses (fired for cause): sometimes vested shares can be repurchased at cost
- Good-leaver clauses (dies, quits with notice, mutually agreed exit): standard treatment

These are uncomfortable conversations to have when co-founding. Have them anyway. The companies that don't end up in bitter, value-destroying disputes when they shouldn't have.

## Hiring / Advisor Equity

### Hiring with equity

Standard ranges (Series A / early stage US):
- VP-level / Senior IC: 0.5-1.5%
- Director / Senior IC: 0.2-0.5%
- IC / Mid-level: 0.05-0.2%

Vesting: 4 years, 1-year cliff, monthly thereafter. ISO when possible (tax-favored). Strike price at 409A.

### Advisor equity

Standard: 0.1-0.5% over 2 years with no cliff (some have 6-month cliff). FAST agreement (Founder/Advisor Standard Template) is a clean default.

### Watch for "death by 1000 cuts"

Each individual grant feels small (0.5% here, 1% there). Total dilution from poor option-pool management can devastate founder equity. Model total dilution before each hire.

## Acquisition / Exit Deals

When a deal is on the table to sell the company, partial-sell, or take a secondary:

### Engage M&A counsel before the term sheet

Not after. The term sheet is where the deal economics are mostly set. M&A counsel costs are trivial relative to the deal size.

### Run a process

If at all possible. A single bidder gives you no leverage. Two bidders gives you significant leverage. Three+ bidders maximizes outcome.

### Earn-outs are usually traps

Sellers project earn-out targets they'll hit. Buyers structure the post-acquisition org / metrics in ways that make targets harder. Most earn-outs miss. If forced to take earn-out, demand:
- Clear, objective, measurable targets
- Control over the variables that affect the targets (don't let the buyer slow your sales hiring, then deny earn-out because revenue didn't grow)
- Acceleration on certain triggers (buyer change of strategy, key person departure)

### Founder rollover equity

Often the buyer wants the founder to roll some equity into the acquirer. This is a future bet on the acquirer. Treat it like any investment decision — diligence the acquirer.

### Lock-ups and non-competes

The deal often includes post-close lock-ups on stock and non-competes restricting your future activity. Negotiate scope, length, and carve-outs aggressively.

## Founder Traps to Avoid

1. **The "fair" deal that's actually unfair**: investor frames their term as "market standard" when it's not. Always validate against actual market data.
2. **The "we'll fix it in the next round"**: Bad terms compound. The next round of investors inherits and often enhances bad terms from the prior round. Fix it now.
3. **The "we trust them"**: Trust is great. Vesting, vetoes, and contracts are also great. Trust isn't a substitute for structure.
4. **The "I don't want to seem difficult"**: Sophisticated investors EXPECT founders to push back. Founders who don't push back on term sheets are seen as naive, not nice.
5. **The "deal momentum" pressure**: Investor says we need to close by Friday. Resist artificial urgency. Real deals can withstand a 1-week delay.
6. **The vague founder agreement**: Co-founders verbally agree on roles, splits, and what happens if X. Months later, X happens. Verbal agreements don't survive contradicting interests. Get it in writing.
7. **The forgotten option pool**: Negotiating valuation pre-money without specifying option pool position. "Pre-money option pool" comes out of founders. "Post-money option pool" comes out of all shareholders. Specify.
8. **The exploding offer**: 24-hour expiration on a term sheet. Almost always artificial pressure. Sleep on it. Real investors will wait.
9. **The "no-shop" trap**: signing a 90-day no-shop with one investor and losing your optionality during negotiation. Limit to 30-45 days max.
10. **The non-compete in employment agreement**: founders signing employment agreements with broad non-competes that restrict their next venture. Negotiate scope.

## Quick reference

| Term sheet term | Founder-friendly | Toxic |
|---|---|---|
| Liquidation pref | 1x non-participating | 2x+ participating |
| Anti-dilution | Broad-based weighted avg | Full ratchet |
| Board | Founder + investor + indep | Investor majority |
| Vesting | 4yr / 1yr cliff / double trigger | No vesting OR single trigger acceleration (founder-favorable but investors hate) |
| Pro-rata | Major investors only | Every investor |
| Pay-to-play | Yes (company-friendly) | No |
| Drag | Reasonable threshold (>50% of preferred + board) | Single investor can trigger |
| Protective provisions | Major events only | Operating decisions covered |
| No-shop | 30-45 days | 90+ days |
| Valuation | Comp-supported | Vibes-based |

## For the user specifically

The user is likely to encounter equity negotiations in:
- **Founding ventures** (e.g. cross-border real estate ventures) — founder splits, partner equity, investor terms
- **A new operating company** (potential pivot) — co-founder splits, advisor equity, possibly fundraising
- **Acquisitions / partial sales** of a client portfolio or service business
- **Equity-for-service deals** with partners (generally avoid taking equity in clients unless conviction is high)

Common traps:
- Don't take token equity in things you don't believe in just to be "in" — small percentages of mediocre ventures clutter your cap-table mental model
- Don't undervalue your own contribution in founder splits — operator-with-AI is more valuable now than 5 years ago
- Demand vesting even when partnering with friends — especially when partnering with friends
- For foreign ownership structures (e.g. Indonesia), get the cap-table on paper with the local entity (PT PMA) + lawyer involvement before scaling
- For US-incorporated ventures, set up 83(b) elections within 30 days of any equity issuance
- For QSBS (Section 1202) — if any US venture might exit, structure for QSBS treatment from day 1 (C-corp, 5-yr hold, etc.)
