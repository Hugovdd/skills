# Philosophy: The Unified Framework

This is the meta-frame. Read it first when invoked. The tactics in `voss-tactics.md`, `trump-tactics.md`, `fisher-frame.md`, and `wisdom-layer.md` are tools — this file tells you which tool to reach for and when.

---

## The 10 Universal Laws

These hold across every school. If you violate any of them, no tactic saves you.

### 1. Never negotiate without a BATNA.

BATNA = Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. The thing you'll do if this deal dies. If your BATNA is "I lose everything," you don't have leverage — you have a hostage situation, and you're the hostage. Fix your BATNA *first*, then negotiate.

**How to fix a weak BATNA:** generate optionality before the conversation. Multiple offers. Multiple buyers. Multiple suppliers. A clear plan for "I'll do it myself / build it myself / live without it." Strong BATNA is the source of all calm in a negotiation.

### 2. The party with less time pressure wins.

If you need this closed by Friday and they don't, you will leak value every hour you talk. Either fix your time pressure (delay, parallel options, walk timer) or accept that the price of speed is real and pay it consciously.

Tactical move: hide your time pressure. Never let them know your deadline.

### 3. The first specific number anchors. Everyone else negotiates against it.

Whoever sets the first concrete figure (price, equity %, valuation, term length) defines the playing field. The other party adjusts up or down from THAT number, not from their original target.

- If your information is strong: anchor first, anchor aggressively, defend with reasons.
- If your information is weak: extract their anchor first, then react.
- Round numbers feel like estimates ("$50K"). Specific numbers feel like analysis ("$47,500"). Use specific numbers — they imply you've done math (Ackerman model, see Voss).

### 4. Concessions are signals. Make them deliberate, reciprocal, and shrinking.

Every concession communicates something about how you value the deal. Rules:
- Never concede without getting something back. Even a small thing.
- Make concessions smaller each round (shows you're approaching your floor).
- Concede on what's cheap to you but expensive to them, and demand what's cheap to them but expensive to you. (This is the entire Fisher "expand the pie" doctrine.)
- Slow your concessions over time. A fast concession looks like you had margin you were hiding.

### 5. Position vs. Interest.

Positions are *what they say they want* ("$50K and ownership of the IP"). Interests are *why they want it* ("I need to show my partner I got a win, and I need the IP because I'm worried you'll go to a competitor").

Always negotiate over interests. The position is the tip of the iceberg. The interest is the lever.

Voss calls this "the Black Swan" — the hidden information that, if you had it, would change everything.

### 6. People decide with emotion and justify with logic.

Even the most "rational" counterparty is operating on fear, pride, anger, greed, or relief. You will win 10x more deals by managing their emotional state than by being right.

Voss's tactical empathy (mirror, label, calibrated question) is built on this single insight. Use it.

### 7. The relationship-value frame dictates the tactic.

Before you choose a tactic, ask: **does the relationship need to survive this deal?**

| Relationship value | Default school | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic / repeat / partnership | Fisher + Voss | Preserve, build trust, expand pie |
| One-time, but they have a network | Voss (lean toward principled) | Reputation effect; don't burn casually |
| One-time, faceless / anonymous | Trump-flavor + Voss tactics | Maximize extraction within reason |
| Adversarial / already burned | Trump + legal posture | The relationship is already dead — use leverage |

Default: lean Voss/Fisher. Escalate to Trump only when the leverage AND relationship math both justify it.

### 8. Silence is a weapon.

After you've made your move, shut up. The next person to speak loses ground. This applies in email too — short, declarative, then nothing. Don't fill space. Don't apologize for the pause. Don't pre-concede.

In email, this means: send the message, then *don't follow up for 48 hours*. Don't soften the next day with "just checking in!" That's free leverage gone.

### 9. Every email is evidence.

Written negotiations become legal record. Assume every email could be exhibited in court or forwarded to their lawyer / your lawyer. This affects:
- Tone (no insults, no slurs, no threats that aren't backed)
- Specificity (don't put a number in writing you can't defend)
- Acknowledgments (don't acknowledge wrongdoing you haven't actually committed)
- Promises (don't promise what you might walk back)

A useful rule: would you be comfortable if this email were read aloud to a judge? If not, rewrite it.

### 10. The best deal is sometimes no deal.

Most lost negotiations weren't lost in the room. They were lost the moment you agreed to negotiate over the wrong thing. If the deal as structured doesn't pass the wisdom-layer pre-check (margin of safety, asymmetric upside, fits your decade), the right move is "I won't be moving forward with this," delivered cleanly and without apology.

Walking away is *the* original leverage move. Whoever can walk wins.

### Quick decision rules

- **By default, you're in Voss mode** (tactical empathy). It works in 80% of deals. It rarely backfires.
- **Switch to Trump mode** when leverage is dramatically in your favor AND the relationship is already low-trust or one-time. Trump tactics in a relationship-driven negotiation will burn you.
- **Pull Fisher to the front** when you sense the surface fight is hiding a Pareto-better trade — when you're both arguing over a single number and missing that the structure has 5 other levers (timing, scope, exclusivity, options, payment schedule).
- **Pull Wisdom to the front** when the user is *emotionally invested* in winning a specific deal that probably shouldn't even happen. Naval's "play long-term games with long-term people" and Buffett's "say no to almost everything" are the antidote to deal-fever.

## The Voss/Trump/Fisher dialectic

These three schools genuinely disagree. Understand the disagreement.

| | Voss | Trump | Fisher |
|---|---|---|---|
| **View of counterparty** | Human under pressure; understand them | Opponent to dominate or escape | Partner with a different perspective |
| **Default move** | Empathize, then redirect | Anchor high, fight back | Separate people from problem |
| **On compromise** | "Never split the difference" — find the third option | Don't compromise unless you have to | Expand the pie before splitting |
| **On emotion** | Acknowledge it ("labels") to defuse | Use it as leverage (intimidation, walkouts) | Manage it, but stay rational |
| **On information** | Probe relentlessly with calibrated questions | Posture; assume you'll bluff better | Share interests to find joint gains |
| **Risk** | Can become formulaic; counterparty learns the tells | Can blow up relationships and deals | Can be naive when the other side is operating in bad faith |

The synthesis to run:
- **Use Fisher's frame** to make sure you're negotiating over the right thing (interests, not positions; explore trades before fighting over a number).
- **Use Voss's tactics** as your conversational and email default — they're empirically the best tools for any single message.
- **Use Trump's leverage doctrine** as a check on naivete — if the other side is playing hardball, don't bring a knife to a gunfight.
- **Use Wisdom (Naval/Buffett/Munger/Dalio)** as the standing veto on bad deals.

## User-specific philosophy notes

Based on the user's stated priorities, many operators are wired to be agreeable, fair, and to want both sides to win. That orientation is a strength in repeat-game deals (long-term clients, partners, team) and a weakness in one-shot or adversarial deals (vendors trying to take advantage, real estate counterparties stalling on closing, contract counterparties redlining in their favor by default).

**Your job as the skill:** detect when an agreeable default is leaving money on the table or signing bad terms, and surface the harder play.

Specific failure modes to watch for in the user's deals:
1. **Fairness fallacy**: assuming the other side is acting in good faith when behavior says otherwise. Counter: ask "what would a cynical observer think their behavior signals?"
2. **Pre-conceding**: offering a discount, flexibility, or scope expansion *before* the counterparty asks. Almost always costs the user money. Counter: make them ask. Make them ask twice.
3. **Speed bias**: wanting deals closed because the open thread feels like a tax. Counter: budget time for negotiation. A 1-week delay can be worth $10K easily.
4. **Reputation tax**: refusing to push hard because "I want to be the good guy." This is real and sometimes correct. But name it. If protecting reputation is worth $X to the user, the math should be explicit, not implicit.
5. **Single-issue framing**: arguing over price/equity % when the actual asymmetric trade is in another dimension (term length, exclusivity, IP carve-outs, payment timing). Counter: always ask "what else is on the table?"

## When to break the rules

Every framework has edge cases. Times when the obvious move is wrong:

- **The relationship multiplier**: A deal that costs the user $20K but cements a 5-year partnership worth $500K is a great deal. Don't optimize the deal in isolation.
- **The reputation flywheel**: A deal that the user loses but loses *with grace* in front of the right audience is sometimes a win. (Naval: "Reputation compounds.") Don't burn a deal to win a battle.
- **The learning bet**: A deal the user takes at a bad price because they want the rep / experience / network is fine — IF that's a conscious choice. Not fine if it's a rationalization for being scared.
- **The asymmetric bet**: A deal with limited downside and uncapped upside (equity in something, an option, a free shot at scale) deserves more aggression than a linear deal. Don't apply the same risk lens to an option that you apply to a contract.

Know which game you're playing before you choose your tactic.

## The single most important question

Before any negotiation, write the answer to this on paper:

> **"What does GOOD look like here, in 5 years?"**

Not what does winning look like today. What does winning look like once this deal compounds into the rest of the user's life? The 5-year version is the one that should drive every tactical choice. The today version is almost always too narrow and too anxious.

If you can't answer that question, you're not ready to negotiate. Stop and do the thinking first.
