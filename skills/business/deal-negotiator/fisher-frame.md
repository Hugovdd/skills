# Roger Fisher / Harvard PON — Principled Negotiation

Source: *Getting to Yes* (Fisher, Ury, Patton, 1981/2011) and the broader Harvard Program on Negotiation (PON) body of work. Fisher founded PON; the school dominates business-school and diplomatic negotiation pedagogy. The framework was developed from international diplomacy (US/Iran hostage release, Camp David accords) and adapted into commercial deals.

This is the *strategic frame*. Voss is what you do in the room. Fisher is the structure of the room itself.

---

## The Core Insight

Most negotiations get stuck because both parties argue over **positions** (their stated demands) instead of over **interests** (the underlying needs the positions are trying to satisfy).

Position-based bargaining:
- Anchors both sides into stated demands
- Frames the deal as zero-sum (whatever you win, I lose)
- Damages relationships through positional warfare
- Produces sub-optimal outcomes — both sides leave value on the table

Principled negotiation (Fisher's alternative):
- Focuses on underlying interests
- Generates options for *mutual* gain before deciding
- Uses objective criteria to choose among options
- Preserves relationship by separating the people from the problem

## The Four Principles

### Principle 1: Separate the People from the Problem

Negotiations involve two parallel tracks: (a) the substantive issue, and (b) the relationship between the people negotiating. If you mix them, you damage both.

**The trap:** Counterparty's tone or behavior makes you angry. You start fighting *the person*, not solving *the problem*. Now you're locked in a personality conflict that hijacks the substantive negotiation.

**The discipline:**
- Talk about your perceptions, not theirs. "I feel concerned when this happens" not "You always do X."
- Validate their emotions before pushing on substance (this is where Voss's labels intersect with Fisher).
- Make the relationship a *shared* asset that both sides want to protect.
- When the counterparty attacks you personally, deflect to the substance: "I want to understand the problem, not the personalities."

**Email application:** When their email lands hostile or pointed, do NOT reply to the hostility. Reply to the underlying substance. "I hear your frustration. Here's how I'm thinking about the substantive issue you raised…" The hostile email becomes a Fisher-style refocusing opportunity.

### Principle 2: Focus on Interests, Not Positions

Position = what they say they want. Interest = why they want it.

**Classic Fisher example:** Two sisters arguing over an orange. Both want the orange. They split it 50/50. Turns out one wanted the peel for baking, the other wanted the juice. Position-based negotiation got each 50% of what they wanted. Interest-based negotiation would have gotten each 100%.

**The discipline:**
- For every stated position, ask: WHY do they want this? (Interest = the answer.)
- Often, multiple interests sit behind one position. Surface them all.
- Your own interests should be examined too — many people don't actually know what they really want; they only know what they're demanding.
- The most powerful interests are usually: security, money, recognition, control, fairness, autonomy.

**Calibrated questions for surfacing interests (intersect with Voss):**
- "What about this is important to you?"
- "Help me understand why this matters."
- "If we got [their stated position], what does that allow you to do?"
- "What would have to be true for you to feel good about this?"

**The unlock:** Once interests are on the table, you often discover that the surface positions are incompatible BUT the interests are compatible. That's where the trade space opens.

### Principle 3: Invent Options for Mutual Gain (Expand the Pie)

Most negotiators move directly from "what do I want" to "what should I demand." Fisher inserts a step: **brainstorm options before committing to any.**

The trap: arguing over a single option (price, equity %, terms) and missing that 5 other levers exist and would create better trades.

**Common levers in any deal (the trade space):**
- Price / amount
- Timing (when paid, when delivered, when closed)
- Term length / commitment duration
- Scope (what's in, what's out)
- Exclusivity / non-compete carve-outs
- Performance contingencies / milestones
- Options / warrants / future rights
- IP ownership / licensing terms
- Termination conditions / cure periods
- Risk allocation (warranties, reps, indemnities)
- Relationship benefits (introductions, references, marketing rights)

**The discipline:** Before locking on a single counter-offer, brainstorm 5-10 structural variations. Each variation trades one lever for another. The pareto-better deal almost always exists in one of those structures.

**Practical move:** "Before we lock numbers, can we explore the shape of the deal a bit? I want to make sure we're optimizing the structure, not just the price."

**For the user:** This is where most deals leak value. The natural style of many operators is to argue the headline number. The 5-year leverage almost always sits in the OTHER terms.

### Principle 4: Insist on Using Objective Criteria

Don't argue over wills — argue over external standards.

**The trap:** "I want $50K" vs "I'll pay $30K" is a will-vs-will fight. Both sides dig in. The "winner" is whoever has more patience or leverage in the moment.

**Fisher's alternative:** Anchor on objective standards. Examples:
- "Comparable deals in the last 12 months closed at $42K" (market comp)
- "Industry standard for this scope is 15-20% margin" (industry norm)
- "Three similar properties on this street sold at $X/sqm" (market data)
- "Standard founder vesting is 4 years with 1-year cliff" (precedent)
- "Bureau of Labor Statistics median salary is $X" (objective data)
- "What the previous owner / employee / vendor received" (internal precedent)

When you frame your ask as "this is what the market / industry / precedent supports," you've moved from "my will vs your will" to "let's both agree to defer to the external standard." That's a much easier negotiation to win because the counterparty can save face by deferring to data, not to you.

**Discipline:** Before any number lands in a negotiation, have a documented basis for it. The number stands on its source. "I think you're worth $X" is weaker than "Comparable scope at companies your size pays $X — here's the data."

## BATNA: The Most Important Concept Fisher Gave Us

**BATNA = Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement.** What you'll do if this deal doesn't happen.

Your BATNA is your floor. You should NEVER accept a deal worse than your BATNA. Your BATNA is also your real leverage — the better your BATNA, the more comfortable you are walking, the more power you have in the room.

### Three BATNA disciplines:

1. **Always have one.** Before any negotiation, articulate your BATNA in writing. "If this deal dies, I will [specific action]." If your BATNA is vague ("I guess I'll figure it out"), you don't have one — you have a hostage situation.

2. **Improve your BATNA before you negotiate.** Most leverage gains come from improving your alternative, not from being cleverer in the room. Generate competing offers. Build the in-house option. Line up the backup vendor. Make the deal *not the only path*.

3. **Estimate their BATNA.** What's the deal worth to them vs. their alternative? You can't negotiate effectively without modeling this. If their BATNA is much worse than yours, you have huge leverage (use it carefully). If theirs is much better, you need to either improve yours or accept reality.

### ZOPA — Zone of Possible Agreement

ZOPA = the range between your reservation price (worst acceptable) and their reservation price (worst acceptable to them). Any deal in the ZOPA is a deal both can accept.

- If their max ≥ your min, ZOPA exists, and the question is *how to split the surplus*.
- If their max < your min, no ZOPA exists. No deal is rational. Either improve your BATNA, change the deal structure, or walk.

**Practical move:** Estimate ZOPA before negotiating. If you suspect there's no ZOPA, your job is to either expand it (by introducing new dimensions — Principle 3) or recognize early that no deal is the right outcome.

## When negotiations get hostile (the Fisher response)

Fisher's specific advice for when the counterparty refuses to play principled negotiation:

### "Don't push back. Side-step."

When they attack:
- They attack you → reframe as "you must really care about this problem"
- They attack your idea → ask "what concerns you about it?"
- They make a positional demand → ask "how did you come up with that number? What would it solve for you?"
- They threaten → label it and ask "if we can't resolve this, what's actually best for both of us?"

The goal: never engage their hostile move on its own terms. Side-step into a Fisher question that redirects to interests, options, or criteria.

### "Negotiation jujitsu"

Fisher's metaphor: when they push, don't push back — pull them in the direction they're pushing.

If they say "this number is final," ask "if it's final, what made you arrive at that number? Help me understand the math." Their justification becomes the next thing you negotiate over.

If they say "we can't do that," ask "what would have to be true for you to be able to?" Now they're problem-solving with you.

## The Fisher communication discipline

Most negotiation failures are communication failures. Fisher's discipline:

### 1. Listen actively.

Repeat what you heard, in your own words, before responding. "Let me make sure I have this — you're saying X because Y, and the implication is Z. Is that right?" (Note: this overlaps with Voss's "that's right" move.)

### 2. Speak to be understood.

- Speak in concrete terms, not abstractions.
- Use "we" framing where possible — make it joint.
- Avoid blame language.
- Speak about specifics, not generalizations.

### 3. Speak about yourself, not them.

"I feel frustrated when deadlines slip" — not "You always miss deadlines."

The first is hard to argue with (it's about your experience). The second is an attack (it's about their behavior).

### 4. Acknowledge before you push back.

"I hear that the timeline is hard on your side. Given that, here's how I'd want to handle it…" The acknowledgment doesn't cost you anything and it lowers their defenses.

## When Fisher fails

Principled negotiation has known failure modes:

1. **The counterparty is operating in bad faith.** They lie, threaten, or change positions arbitrarily. Fisher's principles assume good-faith engagement. When good faith is gone, you need Voss-tactical (to surface what's actually happening) or Trump-leverage (to make bad faith expensive for them).

2. **The leverage asymmetry is massive.** When you have huge leverage, Fisher's "fair criteria" can leave money on the table. When they have huge leverage, Fisher's "let's find a joint gain" can become "let's find ways for me to give in." Adjust.

3. **Cultural mismatch.** Principled negotiation is American/Western default. In many cultures (parts of Asia, Middle East, Latin America), positional bargaining is the expected protocol and "going straight to interests" reads as naive. Calibrate.

4. **The other side has done one round of Fisher training and is now using it as a tactic.** They'll claim "let's focus on interests" while pushing only their interest. Watch for it.

## User calibration

Fisher works exceptionally well for the user in:
- Partner / equity negotiations (co-founders, future partners)
- Long-term client relationships
- Vendor relationships with multi-year value
- Any negotiation where the user and the counterparty are aligned long-term but disagree on a specific term

Fisher is the wrong default for:
- Hostile counterparties acting in bad faith
- One-shot real-estate or vendor closings where the counterparty has no future value
- Cross-cultural deals where positional bargaining is the local norm (use Fisher *as your frame* but expect them to start with a position; meet them with one)

## Quick reference

| Situation | Fisher Move |
|---|---|
| Both sides locked on stated positions | Ask "what's the interest behind that?" |
| Arguing over one variable (price) | Brainstorm 5+ other levers; expand the pie |
| Counterparty attacks personally | Separate people from problem; reply to substance only |
| Numbers thrown out without basis | Demand objective criteria; cite market / precedent |
| Stuck in will-vs-will fight | Reframe as "what objective standard should we use?" |
| You're about to negotiate | Write your BATNA in 1 sentence. Estimate theirs in 1 sentence. |
| Counterparty using tactics on you | Recognize, label, redirect to interests |
| You don't actually know what you want | Pause. List your own interests first, then your position |
